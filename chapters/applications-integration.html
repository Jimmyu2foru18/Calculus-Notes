Applications of Integration

Chapter 5 of Calculus Foundations. Integration is not just a mechanical process — it is a tool for measuring accumulation, area, and volume. In this chapter we apply the definite integral to compute geometric quantities that cannot be found by elementary geometry alone. 5.1 — Area Between Curves

The definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ computes the signed area between the graph of $y = f(x)$ and the $x$-axis. When two curves $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$ enclose a region, subtracting their integrals yields the area of the region between them.

Key Definition

Definition — Area Between Two Curves

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(x) \ge g(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$, the area of the region bounded by $y = f(x)$, $y = g(x)$, $x = a$, and $x = b$ is:

$$A = \int_a^b [\,f(x) - g(x)\,]\,dx$$

The height of a typical vertical strip is $f(x) - g(x)$; the width is $dx$.

When the curves intersect at $x = a$ and $x = b$, these become the limits of integration.

Worked Example — Area Between a Parabola and a Line

Example 5.1.1

Find the area of the region bounded by $y = x^2$ and $y = x + 2$.

Step 1: Find the intersection points by solving $x^2 = x + 2$.

$$x^2 - x - 2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (x-2)(x+1) = 0$$ So $x = -1$ and $x = 2$. These are the limits of integration.

Step 2: Determine which curve is on top on $[-1, 2]$.

Test $x = 0$: $x^2 = 0$ and $x + 2 = 2$, so $x + 2 \ge x^2$. The upper curve is $f(x) = x + 2$ and the lower curve is $g(x) = x^2$.

Step 3: Set up the integral.

$$A = \int_{-1}^{2} \big[(x+2) - x^2\big]\,dx$$

Step 4: Evaluate.

$$A = \left[\frac{x^2}{2} + 2x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{2}$$ $$= \left(2 + 4 - \frac{8}{3}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{2} - 2 + \frac{1}{3}\right)$$ $$= \left(6 - \frac{8}{3}\right) - \left(-\frac{3}{2} + \frac{1}{3}\right)$$ $$= \frac{10}{3} + \frac{11}{6} = \frac{20}{6} + \frac{11}{6} = \frac{31}{6}$$

Answer: The area is $\boxed{\dfrac{31}{6}\text{ square units}}$.

Worked Example — Area Between a Sine Curve and the x-Axis

Example 5.1.2

Find the area enclosed by $y = \sin x$ and the $x$-axis on $[0, 2\pi]$.

Step 1: Sketch or note that $\sin x \ge 0$ on $[0, \pi]$ and $\sin x \le 0$ on $[\pi, 2\pi]$.

Step 2: Split the integral at the zero $x = \pi$.

$$A = \int_0^{2\pi} |\sin x|\,dx = \int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,dx + \int_{\pi}^{2\pi} (-\sin x)\,dx$$

Step 3: Evaluate each piece.

$$\int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,dx = [-\cos x]_0^{\pi} = -(-1) - (-1) = 2$$ $$\int_{\pi}^{2\pi} (-\sin x)\,dx = [\cos x]_{\pi}^{2\pi} = 1 - (-1) = 2$$

Answer: Total area $= \boxed{4}$ square units.

Integrating with Respect to y

Horizontal Strips

Sometimes it is easier to integrate with respect to $y$. If the curves are given as $x = f(y)$ and $x = g(y)$, with $f(y) \ge g(y)$ on $[c, d]$, then:

$$A = \int_c^d [\,f(y) - g(y)\,]\,dy$$

Use horizontal strips when the region is "tall and narrow" rather than "wide and short."

Practice Problems

  1. Find the area bounded by $y = x^3$ and $y = x$.
  2. Find the area bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$, $y = 0$, and $x = 4$.
  3. Find the area enclosed by $y = \cos x$ and $y = \sin x$ on $\left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$.
  4. Set up (but do not evaluate) the integral for the area bounded by $y = e^x$, $y = e^{-x}$, and $x = 1$.

Study Tips

  • Always sketch the region first. Identifying which curve is on top avoids sign errors.
  • When curves cross, split the integral at every intersection point.
  • For regions described by $x = f(y)$, integrating with respect to $y$ can be simpler.
  • If the curves are given implicitly, solve for $y$ (or $x$) before setting up the integral.

5.2 — Volumes of Revolution (Disks and Washers)

Rotating a plane region about an axis produces a solid of revolution. The definite integral lets us compute the exact volume of such solids by summing the volumes of thin cross-sectional slices. The two most common slicing methods are the disk method (used when the solid has no hole) and the washer method (used when the solid has a hollow core).

Key Formulas

Disk Method (rotation about the x-axis)

If $y = f(x) \ge 0$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, rotating the region under $f$ about the $x$-axis produces a solid whose volume is:

$$V = \pi \int_a^b [f(x)]^2\,dx$$

Each cross-section perpendicular to the $x$-axis is a disk of radius $f(x)$.

Washer Method (rotation about the x-axis)

If $R(x) \ge r(x) \ge 0$ on $[a, b]$, rotating the region between $y = R(x)$ and $y = r(x)$ about the $x$-axis produces a solid with a hole. The volume is:

$$V = \pi \int_a^b \Big([R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2\Big)\,dx$$

Each cross-section is a washer with outer radius $R(x)$ and inner radius $r(x)$.

Worked Example — Sphere via Disk Method

Example 5.2.1

A sphere of radius $R$ can be generated by rotating the semicircle $y = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$ about the $x$-axis. Use the disk method to derive the familiar volume formula $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$.

Step 1: Identify the bounds. The semicircle extends from $x = -R$ to $x = R$.

Step 2: Apply the disk method.

$$V = \pi \int_{-R}^{R} \left(\sqrt{R^2 - x^2}\right)^2 dx = \pi \int_{-R}^{R} (R^2 - x^2)\,dx$$

Step 3: Evaluate the integral.

$$V = \pi \left[R^2 x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-R}^{R}$$ $$= \pi \left[\left(R^3 - \frac{R^3}{3}\right) - \left(-R^3 + \frac{R^3}{3}\right)\right]$$ $$= \pi \left[\frac{2R^3}{3} + \frac{2R^3}{3}\right] = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$$

Answer: The volume of a sphere is $\boxed{\dfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3}$.

Worked Example — Washer Method

Example 5.2.2

Find the volume obtained by rotating the region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$ and $y = 0$ about the line $y = 2$.

Step 1: Identify the bounds in $x$. The curves intersect at $x = 0$ and $x = 4$ (since $\sqrt{4} = 2$ is the intersection with $y=2$ if we consider the region, but actually the region under $y=\sqrt{x}$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$ is rotated). Let's be precise: the region is bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$, $x = 0$, $x = 4$, and $y = 0$.

Step 2: Determine outer and inner radii.

When rotating about $y = 2$, the outer radius is $R(x) = 2 - 0 = 2$ and the inner radius is $r(x) = 2 - \sqrt{x}$.

Step 3: Set up the washer integral.

$$V = \pi \int_0^4 \Big[2^2 - (2-\sqrt{x})^2\Big]\,dx$$

Step 4: Expand and evaluate.

$$V = \pi \int_0^4 \left[4 - (4 - 4\sqrt{x} + x)\right]dx = \pi \int_0^4 (4\sqrt{x} - x)\,dx$$ $$= \pi \left[\frac{8}{3}x^{3/2} - \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^4$$ $$= \pi \left[\frac{8}{3}(8) - \frac{16}{2}\right] = \pi \left(\frac{64}{3} - 8\right) = \pi \cdot \frac{40}{3}$$

Answer: The volume is $\boxed{\dfrac{40\pi}{3}\text{ cubic units}}$.

Practice Problems

  1. Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating $y = x^2$ on $[0, 2]$ about the $x$-axis.
  2. Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$ about the $x$-axis.
  3. Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating $y = \sqrt{x}$ on $[0, 4]$ about the $y$-axis. (Use the disk method with respect to $y$.)

Study Tips

  • Always draw the axis of rotation and label the outer radius $R$ and inner radius $r$.
  • When rotating about a line $y = k$ or $x = k$, the radius is the distance from the curve to the axis: $R = |\text{curve} - k|$.
  • Washers subtract the inner area from the outer area; never forget the square: $\pi(R^2 - r^2)$, not $\pi(R-r)^2$.
  • If the axis of rotation is a coordinate axis, the formulas simplify; otherwise account for the shift.

5.3 — Volumes Using Cylindrical Shells

The cylindrical shell method offers an alternative to disks and washers. Instead of slicing the solid perpendicular to the axis of rotation, we slice it parallel to the axis. Each slice becomes a thin cylindrical shell. When integrated, these shells give the exact volume of the solid.

Key Formula

Cylindrical Shell Method (rotation about the y-axis)

If $y = f(x) \ge 0$ on $[a, b]$, rotating the region under $f$ about the $y$-axis produces a solid whose volume is:

$$V = 2\pi \int_a^b x \cdot f(x)\,dx$$

A typical shell has:

  • radius $= x$ (distance from the $y$-axis)
  • height $= f(x)$
  • thickness $= dx$

The shell volume element is $dV = 2\pi(\text{radius})(\text{height})(\text{thickness})$.

Shell Method (rotation about the x-axis)

When rotating about the $x$-axis, use horizontal strips $x = g(y)$ on $[c, d]$:

$$V = 2\pi \int_c^d y \cdot g(y)\,dy$$

A typical shell has radius $= y$, height $= g(y)$, and thickness $= dy$.

Worked Example — Sphere via Shell Method

Example 5.3.1

Derive the volume of a sphere of radius $R$ by rotating the semicircle $y = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$ about the $y$-axis using the shell method.

Step 1: Identify bounds. The semicircle spans $x \in [0, R]$.

Step 2: Apply the shell formula.

$$V = 2\pi \int_0^R x\sqrt{R^2 - x^2}\,dx$$

Step 3: Use substitution. Let $u = R^2 - x^2$, so $du = -2x\,dx$ and $x\,dx = -\frac{du}{2}$.

$$V = 2\pi \int_{x=0}^{x=R} x\sqrt{R^2 - x^2}\,dx = 2\pi \int_{u=R^2}^{u=0} \sqrt{u} \cdot \left(-\frac{du}{2}\right)$$ $$= \pi \int_0^{R^2} u^{1/2}\,du = \pi \left[\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\right]_0^{R^2}$$ $$= \frac{2\pi}{3}(R^2)^{3/2} = \frac{2\pi}{3}R^3$$

Step 4: Wait — this is only half the sphere! The semicircle $y = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = R$ represents only one quarter of the full circle in the first quadrant. Rotating this quarter-circle about the $y$-axis produces only half a sphere.

To get the full sphere, double the result: $$V = 2 \cdot \frac{2\pi}{3}R^3 = \frac{4\pi}{3}R^3$$

Answer: The volume of a sphere is $\boxed{\dfrac{4\pi}{3}R^3}$.

Worked Example — Shell Method for a Cone

Example 5.3.2

Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region bounded by $y = x$, $y = 0$, and $x = 4$ about the $x$-axis using the shell method.

Step 1: Express $x$ in terms of $y$: $x = y$ on $[0, 4]$ for $y$. The region in terms of $y$ is $x = y$ from $y = 0$ to $y = 4$.

Step 2: Apply the shell method about the $x$-axis.

$$V = 2\pi \int_0^4 y \cdot x\,dy = 2\pi \int_0^4 y^2\,dy$$

Step 3: Evaluate.

$$V = 2\pi \left[\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^4 = 2\pi \cdot \frac{64}{3} = \frac{128\pi}{3}$$

Answer: The volume is $\boxed{\dfrac{128\pi}{3}\text{ cubic units}}$.

When to Use Shells vs. Washers

Comparison Table

Method Best For Strip Direction
Disk Solid with no hole; axis is boundary Perpendicular to axis
Washer Solid with a hole Perpendicular to axis
Shell When washers require solving for the inverse function Parallel to axis

A good rule of thumb: if rotating about the $y$-axis and the function is $y = f(x)$, the shell method avoids solving $x = f^{-1}(y)$.

Practice Problems

  1. Use the shell method to find the volume generated by rotating $y = x^2$ on $[0, 3]$ about the $y$-axis.
  2. Use the shell method to find the volume generated by rotating $y = \sqrt{x}$ on $[0, 4]$ about the $x$-axis.
  3. Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$ about the $y$-axis using shells. Compare with the washer method answer from the previous section.

Study Tips

  • Shells are especially powerful when rotating about the $y$-axis and the function is given as $y = f(x)$ — no inversion needed.
  • Always identify radius, height, and thickness before writing the integral.
  • The shell circumference $2\pi r$ is easy to forget; set up $dV = 2\pi r h\,dx$ explicitly until it becomes automatic.
  • When in doubt, sketch both the region and one representative shell.
